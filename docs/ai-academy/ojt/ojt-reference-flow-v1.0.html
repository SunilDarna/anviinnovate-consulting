OJT Reference Flow

Project: Scheme Setu — a grounded eligibility assistant for government welfare schemes, built for a rural livelihood NGO by a squad of five students under supervision.

This document is the operating manual for one live project, end to end: every phase, every role, every gate, every artifact, and an explicit boundary between what the programme owner does and what the students do. It is written to be cloned — swap the project, keep the machinery.

1 · Why this project was chosen

A live project earns its place if it forces the behaviours a self-paced course cannot produce. This one does, on every axis:

PropertyHow this project delivers it
Real stakeholderAn NGO field team with a real backlog of citizen queries. They push back, change their minds, and have opinions about the UI.
Real consequencesA wrong eligibility answer wastes a poor household's day and travel money. This forces genuine refusal design, citation discipline and human-in-the-loop — not decoration.
Genuine ambiguityThe brief says "help our coordinators answer scheme questions." It does not say how. Students must interview, scope and say no.
Hostile dataGovernment scheme PDFs: scanned pages, multi-column layouts, tables, regional languages, amendments that supersede earlier text.
Runs on their hardwareWhole thing fits on an 8 GB laptop — local embeddings, FAISS, a 3B model or a free-tier API. No paid infrastructure.
Curriculum coverageTouches core units 2, 3, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 — data, SQL, evaluation, prompting, RAG, agents, observability, shipping.
PublishablePublic-interest outcome, safe to open-source, good for admissions and for a government-funding narrative.
The one thing it is not: it is not greenfield-easy. Squads consistently underestimate PDF parsing. That is deliberate — the phase plan front-loads it so the pain arrives in week 2 when there is still time, not week 6.

2 · The brief

This is the entire document the squad receives on day one. One page. No solution, no architecture, no tech stack. Writing this well is your job and it takes about two hours.

Brief · SS-001 · Scheme eligibility support

Partner: [NGO name] · Sponsor: [Programme Director] · Squad: 5 · Window: 6 weeks / 90 h

Situation

We have 34 field coordinators across 6 districts. Each supports roughly 400 households. Households ask which government schemes they qualify for. Coordinators answer from memory, from WhatsApp forwards, and occasionally from a printout that may be two years old. We do not know how often they are wrong. We believe it is often.

What we want

A coordinator, on a cheap Android phone with patchy signal, should be able to ask a question in Telugu or English and get an answer they can trust, with the source, in under 30 seconds.

What we can give you

  • Roughly 60 scheme documents (PDF, mixed quality, some scanned)
  • Two coordinators for 3 hours a week for interviews and testing
  • A log of 300 real questions coordinators have asked our office

How we will judge it

  • Our coordinators use it voluntarily after the project ends
  • It says "I don't know" instead of guessing
  • Our office staff can update it when a scheme changes, without calling anyone

Hard limits

  • No household personal data leaves our premises
  • Running cost after handover must be under ₹2,000/month
  • It must never tell a household they are eligible. It tells a coordinator what the document says.
Why the brief withholds so much: the gap between this page and a working system is where the learning lives. A brief that specifies the architecture converts a live project back into a lab exercise.

4 · Scope boundary — yours vs the students'

The single most common failure in OJT programmes is the boundary blurring: the owner starts solving technical problems, or the students get handed commercial and legal risk they cannot carry. Keep this table on the wall.

Yours — everything outside the code

  • Finding and qualifying the partner
  • MoU, data-sharing agreement, IP and licence terms
  • Writing the brief and agreeing success criteria
  • Pre-flight feasibility — proving it is buildable on an 8 GB laptop before students see it
  • Confirming the corpus legally exists and can be used
  • Hiring, briefing and paying the mentor
  • Repo, CI, secrets, accounts, licences
  • Kill criteria and the decision to pull the plug
  • Handling partner escalation and scope creep
  • Who maintains it after handover
  • Certificates, references, publication
  • The fallback project if the partner disappears

Students' — everything inside the code

  • Interviewing the partner and resolving ambiguity
  • Deciding what is in and out of this 90 h
  • All architecture and technology choices
  • Building the golden evaluation set
  • Writing every line of code and every test
  • Reviewing each other's pull requests
  • Estimating, planning and re-planning sprints
  • Deciding what to cut when they run late
  • Running the field test with real coordinators
  • Handling the injected incident
  • Handover documentation and partner training
  • The postmortem and the demo
Never crosses the line to students: signing anything, holding household personal data, direct commercial negotiation with the partner, paying for infrastructure, or being the sole owner of a production system after they leave.
Never crosses the line to you: technical decisions. If the squad picks a worse retrieval strategy than you would have, that is the mentor's coaching problem, not your override. The one exception is safety — see §14.

6 · Phase 0 — Sourcing and de-risking

You · 4–6 weeks before the cohort · ~20 h · students see none of this

Phase 0 exists to guarantee one thing: on day one, the project is known to be doable. Every hour skipped here costs the squad five.

0.1 Source and qualify the partner 6 h

Qualification checklist — all five must be true, or decline:

TestWhy it matters
A named person will give 3 h/weekA partner who cannot attend demos will reject the deliverable at the end.
The data exists today"We'll get you the documents" means you will still be waiting in week 4.
Real users are reachableWithout a field test the project is a lab exercise wearing a costume.
Failure is survivableNever put students on something the partner's operations depend on.
They can say what "good" meansIf they cannot, you must write it and get it signed.

0.2 Paperwork 4 h

  • MoU — this is a training engagement; the deliverable is provided as-is; no SLA; no liability.
  • Data-sharing agreement — what data, where it may be stored, deletion date, no personal data.
  • IP and licence — decide up front. Recommended: partner gets a perpetual free licence, code is open-sourced under Apache-2.0, students keep public credit.
  • Photo and publication consent — you will want the story later.

0.3 Pre-flight build 8 h — do not skip

You or the mentor builds a deliberately ugly end-to-end version in one day. Not to give the students — to prove the project is not impossible and to find the landmines.

  • Parse 5 of the worst PDFs. Do the scanned ones need OCR? Is Telugu text extractable or is it an image?
  • Does an embedding model that fits in 3 GB retrieve anything sensible on this corpus?
  • Does the free-tier LLM quota survive 300 eval questions per night?
  • Time the full index build on a low-end laptop. If it is over 20 minutes, the corpus needs pre-chunking by you.
Pre-flight is where you cancel. If the corpus turns out to be scanned Telugu images with no usable OCR path, that is a six-week project on its own. Find that out now, renegotiate the brief with the partner, and hand the squad a corpus that has a route through it.

0.4 Set up the rails 2 h

  • Repo with branch protection, no direct pushes to main, 1 peer + 1 mentor approval required
  • CI: lint, tests, and a nightly eval job that posts scores to the PR
  • Issue templates, a project board, a DECISIONS.md for architecture decision records
  • Secrets in a vault the students can read but not export
Phase 0 exit gate. Signed MoU · corpus in hand and inspected · pre-flight prototype returns a sensible answer to at least one real question · repo and CI live · mentor briefed · kill criteria written down. Missing any one → do not start; run Anvi Labs instead.

How the kickoff is run

The mentor facilitates and enforces one rule: the squad may not propose a solution in this meeting. Questions only. Students reflexively pitch an architecture in minute four; that habit is the thing being trained out.

  • Partner narrates a normal working day for a coordinator, uninterrupted, 15 minutes
  • Squad asks questions for 60 minutes; one student is scribe and owns the ambiguity log
  • Partner walks through 10 real questions from their query log, explaining what a good answer looks like
  • Squad reads back their understanding; partner corrects it. This read-back catches most misunderstandings.

The design document

Six sections, no more than eight pages. This is the P1 module (SRS/HLD/LLD) applied for real.

SectionMust answer
Problem restatedIn the squad's own words, with the ambiguities they resolved and how
ScopeWhat is in these 90 h, and an explicit out-of-scope list the partner has seen
ArchitectureComponents, data flow, why this retrieval strategy and not another
Evaluation planWhat metrics, what thresholds, how the golden set is built — before building
Risk registerTop 8 risks with likelihood, impact, mitigation, owner. Must include harm risks.
Sprint planThree sprints, with what gets cut first if they run late
Gate 1 — Design review. Mentor approves or sends back. Approval requires: an out-of-scope list the partner has seen · thresholds set before any code · a harm analysis that names the refusal behaviour · a named first-cut item. No repository access to main until this passes.

8 · Phase 2 — Build the spine

Squad · Weeks 2–3 · 30 h · goal: ugly but end-to-end

The target for sprint 1 is one thin vertical slice: a question goes in, a cited answer comes out, and a score is computed. Quality is explicitly not the goal. Squads that polish retrieval before they have an evaluation harness are the squads that fail Gate 3.

Workstream A — Corpus (2 students)

  • Parse → normalise → chunk, with a fallback OCR path for scans
  • Every chunk carries: scheme ID, section, page, document version, publication date
  • Reproducible: make index rebuilds from raw PDFs, deterministically
  • Quality report: how many pages failed, and which

Workstream B — Golden set (2 students)

  • 80 questions sampled from the partner's 300-question log
  • Each labelled with the partner: correct answer, source page, or "not answerable from corpus"
  • 25% must be unanswerable — this is what tests refusal
  • Held in version control, never shown to the generation prompt

Workstream C — Spine (1 student, the sprint's Tech Lead)

Wires the thinnest possible path: retrieve top-k → stuff into a prompt → generate with citations → return. Plus make eval, which runs all 80 questions and prints recall@5, citation validity and refusal accuracy. Ugly is fine. Working is mandatory.

Why the golden set is built in sprint 1, not sprint 3. Without it, every later decision is taste. With it, "did reranking help?" has an answer. It is also the deliverable the partner values most after handover, because it lets them verify changes themselves.
Gate 2 — Sprint 1 demo. Live, with the partner present, on the mentor's machine not the author's. Must show: a real question answered with a working citation link · make index and make eval running from a clean clone · a baseline score on the board. A demo that only runs on one laptop fails.

9 · Phase 3 — Make it correct

Squad · Weeks 4–5 · 30 h · goal: move the numbers, honestly

Now the squad has a baseline and a harness, every change is a hypothesis with a measurement. The mentor's job this sprint is to refuse any PR whose description does not include a before/after number.

The scope change lands in week 4 — see §15. It is mandatory, and the squad's response to it is a graded outcome, not an inconvenience.
Gate 3 — Quality gate. All thresholds from the design doc met, or a written argument for why the threshold was wrong, accepted by the mentor. An honest miss with analysis passes. A silent miss fails. Scores are read from CI, never from a student's terminal.

The field test is the highest-value hour of the whole project

Three coordinators, real questions, squad watching in silence. They may not explain, hint, or touch the keyboard. Every time a coordinator hesitates, that hesitation is a finding. Expect the squad to discover that their carefully-tuned retrieval is fine and their interface is unusable. That lesson does not exist in any lab.

Handover — the part everyone forgets

  • Runbook: how to restart it, what the alerts mean, who to call
  • Corpus update guide — the partner must be able to add a new scheme PDF without a developer. This was one of their three success criteria.
  • The golden set, and how to re-run make eval after an update
  • A 45-minute live training session with the partner's office staff, delivered by the squad
  • Known limitations, written plainly and handed over in writing
Gate 4 — Acceptance. Partner signs the acceptance note · incident postmortem submitted · handover training delivered · deployed system reachable and inside the cost cap · repo clean with no secrets in history.

11 · Phase 5 — Close-out

You + Mentor · the week after · ~8 h

Mentor

  • Per-student evaluation against the rubric (§17)
  • Reference letter draft, one per student, specific and evidence-based
  • Squad retro → feedback into the next brief

You

  • Countersign evaluations; issue the OJT completion record
  • Decide who maintains it. Options: partner takes it fully · next cohort adopts it as a brownfield project · you archive it and tell the partner plainly. Choose one and tell the partner in writing.
  • Publish: repo, case study, partner quote
  • Debrief the partner; ask for the next brief
The maintenance decision is a reputational risk, not an admin task. An abandoned system that a real NGO started depending on is worse than never having built it. Decide before the squad disperses, and put it in the acceptance note.

Rotating tech lead

Each of the five students is Tech Lead for one sprint block. The lead runs planning, owns the sprint commitment, breaks ties, and reports at the demo. It is the single highest-signal item on the evaluation, because it is the only place a fresher can evidence leading rather than doing. With three sprints and five students, two students co-lead — the mentor pairs the two least confident with a partner.

Mentor discipline: speaks last at standup, absent for the first 20 minutes of retro, never types in the squad's repo. The mentor's leverage is in PR review and 1:1s, not in the room.

14 · Safety design — the one place you overrule the squad

Everywhere else the squad's technical decisions stand. Here they do not. These constraints go into the brief and the mentor enforces them at design review.

RuleReason
Never asserts eligibility. Output is "the scheme document states X (source, page)", plus which stated criteria appear met, unmet or unclear.An eligibility verdict is a decision with legal and financial consequence. The system supplies evidence; the coordinator decides.
Coordinator-facing only. Never deployed directly to households.Keeps a trained human in the loop and matches the partner's own hard limit.
Every claim cited, or the answer is blocked.Uncited output cannot be checked, and an unverifiable answer about entitlements is worse than no answer.
Refuses outside the corpus. Refusal accuracy is a gate, not a nice-to-have.The most likely harm is a confident answer about a scheme not in the documents.
Corpus is version-stamped and dated. Answers show the document date.Schemes change. Stale entitlement information sends people on wasted journeys.
No household personal data, ever. Not in prompts, not in logs.Partner's condition, and the students are not equipped to carry that risk.
Every answer logged for audit.When something goes wrong the partner must be able to see what was said.
These seven rules are also the most transferable thing in the project. A student who has shipped under them can talk credibly in an interview about grounding, abstention and human-in-the-loop — which is exactly what an employer deploying GenAI is worried about.

15 · Injected disruptions

Real projects change under you and break at bad times. Both events are scripted, mandatory, and graded. The mentor delivers them in character and does not warn the squad.

Disruption 1 · Scope change Week 4, Monday, at sprint planning

Script (mentor, relaying the partner): "Two districts we work in are majority Hindi-speaking. Leadership wants Hindi as well as Telugu. Also, coordinators keep asking for it on WhatsApp rather than a website."

What is being assessed: not whether they deliver it. Whether they:

  • Ask what the deadline pressure actually is before agreeing to anything
  • Separate the two asks — Hindi is a small change on an existing pipeline; WhatsApp is a new delivery channel and a new approval process
  • Quantify the trade: "we can do Hindi by cutting reranking; WhatsApp does not fit in 15 h and here is why"
  • Escalate to you rather than silently absorbing it
  • Update the design doc and get the change accepted in writing
The failure mode to watch for is silent agreement. A squad that says "sure" and then misses Gate 3 has learned the wrong thing unless the retro names it.

Disruption 2 · Production incident Week 6, Tuesday, 10:00, unannounced

The break (mentor picks one, the day before the field test):

  • Free-tier LLM quota exhausted — every request 429s (most realistic; forces the local-model fallback)
  • The index file is corrupted and retrieval returns nothing
  • A partner staffer uploaded 12 new PDFs and p95 latency tripled
  • An embedding library minor version bump silently changed dimensions

Required response, in order — this is the graded sequence:

   1 DETECT    Did their own monitoring tell them, or did the mentor have to?
        ▼          (If the mentor had to tell them, that is finding number one.)
   2 COMMUNICATE  Message to the partner within 15 minutes. Plain language,
        ▼              what is broken, what it means, when they will next update.
   3 MITIGATE   Restore service, even degraded. Fallback model, cached answers,
        ▼          or an honest maintenance banner. Service before root cause.
   4 DIAGNOSE   Only now. Root cause, with evidence from logs.
        ▼
   5 FIX + VERIFY  Fix, run the eval suite, confirm no regression.
        ▼
   6 POSTMORTEM  Blameless. Timeline, root cause, what made it slow to detect,
                   two concrete prevention actions. Reviewed at retro.

Most squads jump straight to step 4. Being marked on the order, not the fix, is the lesson.

Squad-level failure (the whole project is behind at Gate 3) is handled by the mentor cutting scope with the partner, not by extending the window. Shipping something small that works, on time, is the professional behaviour being trained. Six weeks is fixed.

Evaluation rubric

Mentor scores each 1–5 with a written evidence line. Weighted; 3.0 required to pass, with no dimension below 2.

DimensionWeightEvidence source
Technical contribution25%Merged PRs, complexity, review comments received
Code review quality10%Reviews given — substance, not "LGTM"
Handling ambiguity15%Ambiguity log, questions at kickoff, scope-change response
Communication15%Standups, demo, partner training session, incident comms

Note what is not weighted: whether the project succeeded. A squad can hit a genuinely hard corpus and ship less than planned; individuals are still assessed on how they worked. Grading students on partner outcomes teaches them to hide bad news.

19 · Contingency and kill criteria

FailureWhenResponse
Partner goes silentAny timeMentor plays proxy stakeholder using the brief and the query log. Project continues; the squad is told. Removes the dependency without wasting the work.
Corpus turns out unusablePhase 0Caught by pre-flight. Renegotiate the brief or decline. This is exactly what Phase 0 is for.
Corpus turns out unusableWeek 2Pre-flight failed. Mentor supplies a pre-parsed subset within 24 h and the squad's scope narrows. Your cost, not theirs.
Partner demands production supportWeek 6You handle it, not the squad. MoU already says as-is. Offer a paid engagement separately.
Squad is 2+ students downAny timeMerge with another squad or cut to the thin slice. Never extend the window.
Real harm surfaces in the field testWeek 6Stop the field test immediately, do not deploy, treat as the incident. Partner informed by you the same day.
Nothing is shippable at Gate 3Week 5Mentor cuts to the smallest honest deliverable — often "search with citations, no generation". Ship that. It is still real.
Kill criteria — written in Phase 0, before anyone is emotionally invested. Kill the project if: no signed data agreement by day −7 · pre-flight cannot retrieve a sensible answer to a single real question · the partner cannot name one person who will attend demos · the deliverable would sit on the partner's critical path. When a project is killed, the squad runs Anvi Labs instead — which is why Anvi Labs must exist before your first real project, not after.

20 · Cloning this for any project
