Honeycomb

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Honeycomb's observability product has a real learning curve: new users struggle to map the mental model of their data and what they want to ask into the Query Builder interface, especially anyone without prior observability tooling experience. This suppressed activation.

Built. Query Assistant, a natural-language-to-query feature. Shipped as an experimental feature to all customers roughly one month after they spotted the opportunity, then iterated daily on production data before promoting it to a core product offering. Deliberately skipped traditional regression testing because of LLM nondeterminism and used SLOs for monitoring instead. Schema embeddings stored in a Redis cluster on AWS.

Stack
OpenAI GPT-3.5-turboOpenAI text-embedding-ada-002Redis cluster on AWSSLO-based monitoring (in place of regression tests)

Outcome. Adoption: 82% of active Enterprise/Pro Plus teams, 75% self-serve, 39% free tier. Week-6 manual query retention 26.5% with the Assistant vs 4.5% without; complex query creation 33% vs 15.7%; board creation 11% vs 3.6%. Running cost roughly $30/month in OpenAI API fees plus ~$100/month per Redis node. Average request time halved and P99 latency fell several times over since the May 2023 launch.

What went wrong
The team's own verdict was 'mostly' worked, and they published the misses. Free-tier adoption was a disappointing 39% despite zero financial barrier - many users simply never noticed the feature sitting below the Query Builder, a pure discoverability failure that no model change would fix, and they responded by rewriting onboarding and nurture emails. Trigger creation, which they expected to correlate with alerting and conversion, came in at 5.8% vs 3.6% - not enough signal. Query Assistant retention itself collapsed after the first week, though on inspection this was users 'graduating' to manual querying rather than churning. They admit they could not predict the variety of natural-language inputs users would supply or how the LLM would answer, which is why they abandoned pre-launch testing for production observability. They also had to aggressively shrink the prompt to stay under OpenAI rate limits and control cost, and chose GPT-3.5-turbo over GPT-4 after finding it adequate with a tuned prompt.
Shrinking it to a laptop
The single best template for a U1 quick-win exercise. Students build a natural-language-to-query feature over a small SQLite or DuckDB dataset with a free-tier LLM in a day, then do the part that actually matters: define the activation metric before shipping, instrument it, and compare cohorts with and without the feature. The $30/month cost figure and the ship-in-one-month cadence are directly imitable. The discoverability failure is reproducible by having half the class hide the feature below the fold and measuring adoption.

LinkedIn

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. LinkedIn wanted generative AI to help members with job assessment, post summarisation and career advice - faster information retrieval and personalised guidance inside the feed and job search.

Built. A RAG pipeline with three stages - routing to specialised agents, retrieval from internal APIs and external sources including Bing, and generation synthesising a response with attachments - fronting dozens of task-specific AI agents. Internal RPC APIs were wrapped in LLM-friendly schemas because the models had never seen them. LLM output was requested as YAML and parsed defensively. Responses streamed end to end, with LLM output parsed progressively so downstream API calls could fire before generation finished. Server-driven UI framework for client updates.

Stack
LLMsEmbedding-Based Retrieval (EBR) with in-memory storesYAML structured output + custom defensive parserBing (external retrieval)server-driven UI frameworkstreaming infrastructure

Outcome. Reached 80% of target quality in one month, then spent four more months getting to 95%. Cut LLM output formatting errors from ~10% to ~0.01% via defensive parsing. Evaluation throughput of up to 500 conversations assessed per day with roughly one-day turnaround.

What went wrong
This is the canonical published account of the last-mile problem: 'Getting it 80% was fast, but that last 20% took most of our work.' Prompt engineering plateaued in effectiveness while continuously creating the false impression they were almost there. Manual annotation could not scale as the evaluation bottleneck, forcing written consistency guidelines across evaluators. LLM YAML output was malformed about 10% of the time, and the obvious fix - re-prompting to correct the format - added unacceptable latency, so they wrote a custom defensive parser instead. Chain-of-thought improved accuracy but multiplied token counts, and their initial time-to-first-token bounds turned out to be the wrong metric: time-between-tokens hurt perceived latency more than they anticipated once reasoning steps stacked up. GPU capacity limits forced them to ration and schedule development work early on.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Fully reproducible on a laptop with a free-tier API. Have students build a small RAG assistant over ~200 documents, get it to 'looks good' in an afternoon, then build a 30-example labelled eval set - the 80/20 wall appears immediately and honestly. The YAML/JSON malformation lesson is the highest-value exercise: log the raw model output for 200 calls, measure the structured-output failure rate, then compare a defensive parser against a re-prompt retry on both correctness and added latency. TTFT vs TBT is measurable with a stopwatch on any streaming endpoint.

Microsoft, Accenture and an anonymous Fortune 100 company

Peer-reviewed paperquality: highreproducible: partialverified

Problem. Firms deploying AI coding assistants had no credible causal estimate of the productivity effect, only self-reported surveys and vendor claims. The question was whether a generative AI tool measurably increases high-skilled knowledge output in real workplaces, not in lab tasks.

Built. Three separate randomized controlled trials at three organisations. A random subset of developers within each firm was granted access to an AI code-completion assistant; the control group was not. Outcomes were measured from real engineering telemetry (completed tasks, pull requests) rather than surveys. Registered in the AEA RCT Registry and published in Management Science.

Stack
AI code-completion assistant (GitHub Copilot-class tool)pull request and task-completion telemetryrandomized controlled trial design

Outcome. Pooled across the three experiments and 4,867 developers, a 26.08% increase in completed tasks for developers with the AI tool (standard error 10.3%). Less experienced developers showed both higher adoption rates and larger productivity gains than senior developers.

What went wrong
The paper is explicit that results vary across the three experiments before pooling - the effect was not uniform across firms, which is the finding most often dropped when this study is cited. The 10.3% standard error on a 26.08% point estimate is wide: the honest reading is a large but imprecisely estimated effect, not a settled 26%. The abstract does not report a code-quality or defect-rate outcome, so no claim about downstream quality is supported by this evidence. The experience heterogeneity also cuts against the common deployment assumption that senior engineers benefit most.
Shrinking it to a laptop
You cannot run a 4,867-developer RCT in a classroom. But the transferable skill is reading a deployment claim correctly, and that is a laptop exercise: give students the point estimate and standard error and have them compute the confidence interval in Python, see that it spans roughly 6% to 46%, and compare that honest range against the vendor headline. Pair it with a small simulation showing how a per-firm effect that varies can pool into a confident-looking average. This is the antidote case for U1 - every other case in this unit reports a number, and this one teaches what a number is worth.

https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535

Klarna

News reportquality: mediumreproducible: yescorrected

Problem. Klarna wanted to cut customer service cost and resolution time across a very high volume of routine support conversations.

Built. An OpenAI-powered customer service AI assistant, launched globally in February 2024, fronting the majority of inbound customer service traffic. Later rebuilt as a hybrid model with escalation paths routing specific case types to human agents, and human premium-support roles re-opened on flexible remote contracts.

Stack
OpenAI modelscustomer service chat channel

Outcome. Klarna's self-reported first-month figures, as relayed by the outlet: 2.3 million chats handled, claimed equivalent to the work of 700 representatives; two-thirds of customer service chats handled by AI; average resolution under 2 minutes; response times 82% improved since launch; a reported 25% drop in repeat issues. (The '11 minutes for humans' and '$40M profit contribution' figures come from Klarna's own Feb 2024 press release, not this source.) In May 2025 CEO Siemiatkowski said the company went 'too far in the wrong direction with AI' and that investing in the quality of human support 'is the way of the future'.

What went wrong
The most valuable public reversal in the U1 space. Klarna optimised on cost and the quality signal degraded: CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski stated that 'As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality,' and that 'Really investing in the quality of the human support is the way of the future for us.' Customer frustration built around impersonal interactions and, specifically, the inability to reach a human at all - a cited Verint survey found over two-thirds of customers had bad chatbot encounters, with inability to answer the question as the top complaint. Note it was not a full rollback: the assistant still handles roughly two-thirds of inquiries. The failure was the missing escalation path and the choice of cost as the primary evaluation metric, not the model.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Sourcing caveat: the launch figures originate from Klarna and OpenAI press material and are not independently audited; this news report covers the walk-back. Reproducible as a design and measurement exercise: students build a small support bot over a 50-article help centre, then are required to define and instrument a deflection metric AND a quality metric AND an escape hatch to a human. Seed the test set with 10 questions the knowledge base cannot answer and show that a cost-only dashboard reports success while every one of those 10 users is stranded. No scale needed - the lesson is entirely about which metric you put on the dashboard.

Air Canada

Other primary sourcequality: mediumreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Air Canada deployed a customer-facing support chatbot on its website to answer passenger questions about fares and policies, including bereavement fares.

Built. A website support chatbot answering policy questions in natural language, apparently without hard grounding in, or reconciliation against, the authoritative policy pages elsewhere on the same site.

Stack
customer-facing website chatbot (specific model/vendor not disclosed in the ruling coverage)

Outcome. In Moffatt v. Air Canada the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation and held Air Canada liable for the chatbot's statement. The tribunal awarded damages to the passenger (widely reported as CAD $812.02 including fees; the legal analysis retrieved confirms damages were awarded without stating the amount).

What went wrong
The chatbot told a grieving passenger he could buy a full-fare ticket and apply retroactively for a bereavement discount. Air Canada's actual published policy, on the same website, said bereavement fares cannot be applied retroactively - so the failure was an ungrounded generative answer contradicting authoritative content the company already had. Air Canada's defence made things worse and is the part worth teaching: it argued the chatbot was effectively a separate entity for whose statements it bore no responsibility, and that the correct information existed elsewhere on its site. The tribunal rejected both, finding a duty of care, a failure of reasonable diligence, and stating it 'should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website.' Having a correct policy page is not a defence when the bot contradicts it.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Source is a law firm's case analysis, reliable on the legal findings but not an engineering account - no stack details exist publicly. Reproduces perfectly as a grounding exercise: give students a 20-page policy corpus containing several near-miss clauses (a refund policy that IS retroactive next to a fare policy that is not), and have them measure how often an ungrounded LLM answer contradicts the source. Then require citation-back-to-source-span and re-measure. Free tier, CPU only, and it produces the single most important U1 habit: a customer-facing bot's answers are the company's answers.

https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/moffatt-v-air-canada-misrepresentation-ai-chatbot

New York City (Mayor's Office / NYC Small Business Services)

News reportquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. NYC wanted to help small business owners navigate city rules and regulations without wading through legal text, and launched an AI chatbot on the MyCity portal in autumn 2023.

Built. A public-facing AI chatbot on Microsoft's cloud platform, answering small business regulatory questions. After press scrutiny the city added disclaimers advising the answers not be treated as legal advice, made targeted answer corrections, and restricted the categories of question the bot would engage with.

Stack
Microsoft Azure cloud platform (specific model not disclosed)

Outcome. Roughly $600,000 to build the chatbot's foundations, with ongoing costs cited by Mayor Mamdani at around half a million dollars annually. The bot was terminated in January 2026 as part of budget cuts and taken down by 4 February 2026, its page reading that the 'beta test has ended.' No accuracy or usage metrics were ever published.

What went wrong
The Markup and THE CITY documented the bot routinely advising businesses to break the law: that landlords could refuse Section 8 housing voucher holders (illegal source-of-income discrimination in NYC), that employers could take a cut of workers' tips, that businesses could refuse cash despite a 2020 city law requiring acceptance, and it stated the city minimum wage incorrectly. The city's response is the instructive part: rather than take it offline, the administration added a disclaimer, patched individual answers, narrowed the question scope, and publicly defended it. The pattern - ship fast into a high-stakes regulated domain, then defend and patch rather than gate - ran for over two years at roughly $500k/year before the project was simply killed with no published accuracy metric to show for it.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Investigative journalism with original testing, which is why quality is high despite not being an engineering blog. The reproduction is an adversarial evaluation exercise and needs nothing but a free-tier API: students build a small regulatory Q&A bot over a public rules corpus, then write 25 adversarial questions where the compliant answer is counterintuitive, and score how many answers would expose a user to legal risk. The key teaching point is that a disclaimer is not a mitigation, and that the correct gate is a refusal-and-escalate path on regulated topics - both fully demonstrable at 25 questions.

McDonald's and IBM

Other primary sourcequality: mediumreproducible: partialcorrected

Problem. McDonald's wanted automated voice order taking at drive-thrus to cut labour cost and speed up service.

Built. The McDonald's Automated Order Taking (AOT) voice AI system, developed with IBM on technology from IBM's 2019 acquisition of Apprente. Piloted from June 2021 at over 100 US drive-thru locations.

Stack
IBM voice AI (Apprente-derived automated order taking)

Outcome. Deployed at 'over 100 U.S. drive-thrus' from 2021 through mid-2024, built on technology from IBM's Apprente acquisition. Documented failure modes included adding unwanted items, mixing adjacent lane orders and ignoring corrections. 'In June 2024, McDonald's confirmed it ended the IBM pilot, citing plans to explore alternative voice-AI vendors.' No accuracy, order-error-rate or ROI figures were ever published. (The 26 July 2024 franchisee shut-off deadline is from separate trade-press reporting on an internal memo, not this source.)

What went wrong
Documented failure modes: the system added unwanted items to orders, picked up and mixed in speech from adjacent drive-thru lanes, could not handle accents and dialects, misheard menu items, and would not accept customer corrections. Widely circulated recordings showed it ordering 260 chicken McNuggets from a request for one, adding bacon to an ice cream order, and adding nine sweet teas instead of one. Two structural lessons: the input environment (open-air, overlapping speakers, road noise, accent diversity) was the actual hard problem and was under-modelled, and the absence of an easy correction or human-handoff path converted every error into a public spectacle. It ran for three years across 100+ sites before being pulled, and McDonald's still framed the pullback as confirming that voice ordering will be part of its future.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Source is the curated AI Incident Database, which aggregates and dates primary reporting; treat the viral order examples as press-sourced rather than audited. Voice ASR fine-tuning is out of reach on 8GB CPU-only, but the load-bearing lesson is testable for free: run a free/offline ASR model (e.g. faster-whisper tiny on CPU) over 30 short menu-order clips recorded with background noise, overlapping speakers and varied accents, and chart word error rate by condition versus clean studio audio. Students then design the correction and escalation path. The lesson - that deployment environment, not model quality, dominated the failure - reproduces exactly at 30 clips.

Microsoft Research India + AI4Bharat (IIT Madras) — "Jugalbandi"

Other primary sourcequality: mediumreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Roughly 89% of India's population does not speak English, but most government scheme documentation exists only in English. Rural citizens could not find out which of ~20,000 welfare programmes they were eligible for, or how to apply.

Built. A WhatsApp bot that chains four hosted models rather than training anything new: user sends voice or text in an Indian language -> AI4Bharat ASR transcribes -> Bhashini/IndicTrans2 translates to English -> Azure OpenAI GPT model retrieves and reasons over a curated corpus of government scheme documents -> answer translated back to the user's language -> AI4Bharat TTS synthesises audio -> returned over WhatsApp. Deliberately built on WhatsApp because that is the app rural users already have.

Stack
WhatsApp Business APIAI4Bharat ASRBhashini / IndicTrans2 machine translationAzure OpenAI Service (GPT)AI4Bharat TTS

Outcome. Launched April/May 2023 starting in Biwan village, Haryana. Covers 171 of roughly 20,000 government programmes, in 10 of India's 22 official languages. No accuracy, latency, or user-retention numbers published.

What went wrong
Microsoft's own writeup concedes the models make mistakes and explicitly frames them as probabilistic machines requiring human verification: "people still play an important role to see what works and what doesn't work." The cascade design means ASR errors, translation errors and LLM errors compound, and no per-stage error budget is published. Coverage of only 171 of ~20,000 schemes is itself the largest unaddressed failure mode — most eligible queries fall outside the corpus. No hallucination-rate measurement is reported.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Reproducible at laptop scale and an excellent U1 exercise. Build the same 5-stage cascade with free-tier or small local models (faster-whisper tiny, a distilled IndicTrans2 checkpoint, any free-tier LLM, a free TTS), over a corpus of 20 scheme PDFs and 50 hand-written Hindi/Telugu queries. The teaching point — measuring where in the chain the answer breaks, and that end-to-end accuracy is the product of stage accuracies — needs 50 queries, not 50 million.

Uber

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Uber processes thousands of global supplier invoices daily across 25+ languages, with wildly varying templates, handwritten text and scanned copies. 15-20 header attributes plus line-item detail had to be captured per invoice; manual extraction was slow, costly and error-prone.

Built. TextSense, a modular document-processing platform. Pipeline: document ingestion (email/ticketing system, self-service supplier portal, manual PDF upload) -> pre-processing -> CV/OCR via Uber's internal Vision Gateway -> LLM extraction -> post-processing -> human-in-the-loop validation -> ERP integration. Orchestrated on Uber's Cadence workflow engine. Designed so new document types onboard through configuration changes rather than new code.

Stack
Uber Vision Gateway (OCR)OpenAI GPT-4Google Flan-T5Meta Llama 2seq2seq modelsUber Cadence (workflow orchestration)TextSense (internal platform)

Outcome. 2x reduction in manual invoice processing; ~90% overall extraction accuracy (35% of invoices at 99.5% accuracy, 65% above 80%); 70% reduction in average handling time; 25-30% cost saving versus manual processing.

What went wrong
Rule-based systems and RPA were tried first and failed: they needed continual manual rule updates and could not absorb new vendor formats. Fine-tuned open-source models were then tried and also failed on the hard part. Fine-tuned T5 hit over 90% accuracy on header fields but collapsed on line items, with a considerable accuracy drop from the second line onward. Fine-tuning actively induced hallucinations, especially on line-item data. Open-source models were good at reading what was on the page but poor at mapping it onto Uber's existing invoice data patterns. They ended up on GPT-4 plus a mandatory human-in-the-loop review stage, and prioritised labelling effort at high-volume suppliers whose field-level accuracy fell below threshold.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Shrink to 30-50 PDF invoices in 3-4 templates plus a few deliberately skewed phone photos. Use pdfplumber for born-digital text, Tesseract for scans, and a free-tier LLM for field extraction into a fixed JSON schema. The core lesson - header fields are easy, repeating line items are where accuracy dies, and fine-tuning can make hallucination worse rather than better - reproduces at 50 documents. Students measure header-level vs line-level accuracy separately, which is the whole point.

Dropbox

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. The Dropbox mobile document scanner produced images only: text in a scanned receipt or invoice was just pixels, so it could not be searched or copy-pasted. Commercial OCR SDKs were priced per use and were tuned for flatbed scanners, not phone photos taken at angles in bad light.

Built. An in-house OCR pipeline: mobile client uploads to an async work queue -> RPC to an OCR service cluster -> MSER-based Word Detector (classic CV) locates words -> Word Deep Net (CNN + bidirectional LSTM + CTC layer with batch norm) transcribes each word -> Wordinator resolves merged and fragmented word boxes -> hidden OCR text layer merged into the PDF -> search indexing. Compute-heavy components sandboxed with LXC, CGroups, Linux namespaces and Seccomp. Ran inference on CPU, not GPU. Roughly 8 months research to deployment.

Stack
Torch (research)TensorFlow (production)OpenCV (MSER word detection, partially rewritten)CNN + BiLSTM + CTCInception ResNet v2 (orientation detection)BLASLXC / CGroups / SeccompStormcrow (experiment framework)DropTurk (internal annotation platform)

Outcome. Single Word Accuracy rose from ~79% on first synthetic training, to high-80s after synthetic data iterations, to mid-90s after fine-tuning on 20,000 real word images. First full end-to-end document-level test scored only ~44%. Final system matched or beat the previous commercial OCR SDK on a blind end-to-end test on user-donated images. Shipped to millions of users.

What went wrong
They first tried a deep-learning word detector (RCNN-style object detection) and abandoned it: documents contain hundreds to thousands of words, orders of magnitude more objects than contemporary detectors handled, so they fell back to classic MSER, partly because it was debuggable where a net was opaque. The bigger shock was the gap between per-word and end-to-end accuracy: mid-90s word accuracy produced only ~44% document accuracy, because of merged words ('helloworld'), fragmented words ('wo rld') and spurious noise boxes. Fixes were CTC confidence scoring with three-tier filtering (accept / reject / validate against an Oxford English Dictionary lexicon) plus the Wordinator. Synthetic training data failed repeatedly in specific ways: receipts printed on thermal printers (fixed by buying ancient thermal-printer fonts), stippled and smudged disconnected characters, under-represented symbols like / and & (fixed by synthesising dates, prices and URLs), and no negative examples for the high-recall detector's empty boxes (fixed with textured wood and marble backgrounds). Naive random font sampling was replaced by 2,000 hand-audited fonts with frequency-weighted sampling. Finally, Apple Preview's own word-boundary heuristics silently merged words in the output PDF, forcing renderer-specific workarounds.
Shrinking it to a laptop
The single most reproducible case in the set. On a CPU laptop: 200 word-crop images, a small CRNN in PyTorch, or even just Tesseract. Give students a system that scores 95% per word and make them measure document-level exact match - they will land in the 40s and have to build the confidence-threshold + dictionary-validation + word-reassembly layer themselves. The synthetic-data failure modes (thermal fonts, missing symbols, no negative examples) all reproduce with PIL-rendered text at a few thousand samples. Do not attempt the 10M-word final training run; the lesson lives entirely in the 44%-to-passing gap.

Grab

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: partialverified

Problem. Grab's eKYC onboarding for merchants, drivers and users across Southeast Asia had to read ID cards, driver's licences and vehicle registration certificates in Bahasa Indonesia, Thai, Vietnamese and English. Document templates were unstandardised and existing systems could not read non-Latin SEA scripts reliably.

Built. A custom vision LLM trained in-house. First iteration was Qwen2-VL 2B with two-stage training (continual pre-training then full fine-tuning). Final production model is a ~1B-parameter hybrid stitching Qwen2-VL 2B's vision encoder to Qwen2.5 0.5B's language decoder, trained in four stages. Supported by Documint, an internal auto-labelling framework with detection, orientation, OCR and key-information-extraction modules, and a synthetic OCR data generation pipeline for SEA scripts. Serves live production traffic.

Stack
Qwen2-VL 2BQwen2.5 0.5BCommon Crawl (SEA language text)Documint (internal auto-labelling framework)synthetic OCR data generationfull-parameter fine-tuning of vision encoder + projector + decoder

Outcome. Thai extraction accuracy improved by roughly 70 percentage points over baseline; Vietnamese by roughly 40 percentage points. The custom 1B model stayed within a 3pp accuracy gap of the 2B model across most document types while being far faster. External LLM APIs showed P99 latency 3-4x their P50, which the in-house model avoided.

What went wrong
Four approaches were tried and rejected before the final design. Traditional OCR could not cope with template diversity. Proprietary LLM APIs had high and unpredictable latency (P99 at 3-4x P50), poor SEA language understanding, and hallucinated. Off-the-shelf open-source vision LLMs were more efficient but not accurate enough for production. Critically, LoRA fine-tuning worked on Latin scripts but still failed on Thai and Vietnamese - the diagnosis was that open-source vision LLMs have broad multilingual text coverage in the decoder's pre-training but almost no visual SEA-script data in the vision encoder's training, so adapter-only tuning could not fix it. Only full-parameter fine-tuning of the vision encoder closed the gap.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Training a vision LLM is impossible on an 8GB CPU laptop. But the load-bearing lesson - that a model can 'know' a language in text while being blind to it visually, so the failure is in the encoder not the decoder - is demonstrable for free. Students run a free-tier multimodal API over rendered text images in Latin script vs Devanagari/Thai/Tamil and chart the accuracy cliff, then generate synthetic script images with PIL and show that the same text as plain input parses fine. The diagnosis skill transfers; the training does not.

Shopify

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Shopify runs Airflow in production for data extraction, ML model training, Apache Iceberg table maintenance and dbt-powered data modelling. Their largest environment carries over 10,000 DAGs, over 400 concurrently running tasks and over 150,000 task runs per day, and it kept degrading in ways that were not obvious from the DAG code.

Built. A set of operational fixes layered on Airflow 2.2 on Kubernetes with a Celery executor and MySQL 8: an in-cluster NFS server with a sync script pulling DAGs from GCS; a 28-day metadata retention DAG that deletes historical DagRuns, TaskInstances and Logs via ORM queries; a YAML namespace manifest registering owner and source repo per DAG namespace; a cluster DAG policy enforcing DAG ID prefixes, allowed Celery queues and permitted Kubernetes namespaces; deterministically randomised cron schedules seeded on a hash of the DAG ID; and Celery queue isolation plus pools synced via a Kubernetes ConfigMap.

Stack
Apache Airflow 2.2KubernetesCelery executorMySQL 8Google Cloud StorageGCSFuse (abandoned)NFSApache Icebergdbt

Outcome. Over 10,000 DAGs, 400+ concurrent tasks and 150,000+ runs per day sustained. Randomised scheduling produced visible load smoothing across a 12-hour window in production. No single headline percentage published for the other fixes.

What went wrong
Six distinct production failures, each documented with the fix and its trade-off. GCSFuse mounting of the DAG bucket caused severe degradation because every file read became a GCS request and every pod mounted independently. Accumulated metadata slowed the web UI and made version-upgrade migrations take hours - the 28-day retention fix cost them the ability to run long backfills. With DAGs deployed from many repos and generated dynamically, nobody could tell which team owned a failing or misbehaving job. DAG authors had broad system access that admins could not manually review at that volume. The most instructive failure: users auto-generated large DAG batches sharing one schedule_interval, and human-written crontabs cluster on round times (hourly, midnight), so everything fired simultaneously and overloaded both the scheduler and downstream external systems. Priority weights turned out to sum with downstream tasks, systematically biasing scheduling toward larger DAGs regardless of importance. They concluded single-environment scaling was the wrong shape and moved toward multiple environments to limit blast radius.

Booking.com

Peer-reviewed paperquality: highreproducible: partialcorrected

Problem. Booking.com had ~150 customer-facing ML applications built by dozens of teams and needed to know whether investment in better models was actually producing commercial value. The specific problem: teams were optimising offline metrics (ROC AUC for classifiers, MRR for rankers) and assuming that improvement would carry through to conversion, cancellations and customer-service contacts.

Built. An organisation-wide practice, not a single system: every model is validated through a randomised controlled trial on their in-house experimentation platform against business metrics, never offline metrics alone. Techniques include a Problem Construction Process that treats the choice of target and observation space as an iteration dimension in its own right; Response Distribution Analysis, a label-free heuristic that histograms the model's output scores to spot pathologies (smooth unimodal = high Bayes error, spike at an extreme = feature-layer bug like wrong scaling, train/serve distribution difference = concept or feature drift) which works despite incomplete and delayed feedback; and sophisticated RCT designs — selective triggering, model-output-dependent triggering with a 3-arm setup, and a 'models disagree' triggering design for model-vs-model comparison. Latency is contained via model redundancy, an in-house linear prediction engine covering everything reducible to inner products (naive Bayes, GLMs, k-NN with cosine/Euclidean, matrix factorisation), sparse models, precomputation into a distributed key-value store, bulked requests and minimum feature transformations.

Outcome. Across 23 comparisons (46 models) where a new model beat an old one offline, the correlation between offline gain and business-metric gain was Pearson -0.1 (90% CI -0.45 to 0.27) and Spearman -0.18 (90% CI -0.5 to 0.19) — i.e. no correlation at all. A 4-arm latency RCT showed 'an increase of about 30% in latency costs more than 0.5% in conversion rate', and four independent latency-reduction experiments were all statistically significant. Model-family business impact is published only as a relative bar chart (Figure 2) against a median baseline — no numeric values are given in the text. Semantic (general-purpose) models generated on average twice as many use cases as specialised ones.

What went wrong
The headline negative result is that better offline metrics did not translate into business value. They name four mechanisms: value-performance saturation (the value curve flattens, further gains are undetectable in a reasonable-length RCT); segment saturation (as models converge, the disagreement rate falls, so triggered analysis has fewer exposed users and less power); the uncanny valley, where accurate predictions unsettle users and hurt value (documented with a destination recommender using Markov chains that produced public complaints); and proxy over-optimisation, where a CTR-optimised recommender learned to surface hotels very similar to the one being viewed, driving clicks but drowning users in choice and hurting conversion.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Canonical source is KDD '19, ACM DOI 10.1145/3292500.3330744 (this is a mirror of the same PDF). You cannot run an RCT on hundreds of millions of users from a laptop. But the core lesson is reproducible in simulation and is one of the highest-value exercises available: on any 10k-row tabular set, define a business metric that is a noisy non-monotonic function of the model score, train five models with monotonically improving AUC, then plot AUC gain against simulated business gain and watch the correlation vanish. Response Distribution Analysis is directly reproducible — histogram predict_proba output on CPU and inject a scaling bug to see the tell-tale spike at 0 or 1.

Facebook (Meta)

Peer-reviewed paperquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Predicting click-through on ads for 750M daily active users and 1M+ active advertisers, where ads are not tied to a query but to demographic and interest targeting, so the candidate set per request is far larger than in sponsored search. The final-stage classifier must be accurate and well calibrated, because auction bidding depends on the absolute predicted probability, not just the ranking.

Built. A hybrid model: gradient-boosted decision trees (Gradient Boosting Machine, L2-TreeBoost) used purely as a supervised feature transform — each tree contributes a categorical feature whose value is the index of the leaf the instance lands in, 1-of-K encoded into a sparse binary vector — feeding a probabilistic sparse linear classifier trained online with per-coordinate-learning-rate SGD. Compared against Bayesian online probit regression (BOPR). Around this sits the 'online joiner': a distributed stream-to-stream join of the impression stream and the click stream keyed on request ID, using a HashQueue (FIFO buffer plus hash map) with a tuned waiting window; impressions unjoined when the window expires are emitted as negative examples. Output goes to Scribe and feeds a continuously retraining Trainer that periodically publishes models to the Ranker.

Stack
Gradient Boosting Machine (L2-TreeBoost)logistic regression with per-coordinate SGDBayesian online probit regression (BOPR)ScribeHashQueue (custom FIFO + hash map)normalized entropy / calibration as evaluation metrics

Outcome. Tree feature transforms reduce normalized entropy by more than 3.4% relative to no tree transforms — against a background where typical feature-engineering work moves NE by a fraction of a percent. Relative NE with Trees-only as 100% reference: LR + Trees 96.58%, LR-only 99.43%. Going from weekly to daily retraining improves NE by approximately 1% for both model types. Per-coordinate learning rate beats per-weight by nearly 5% NE. BOPR lands at 99.82% NE relative to per-coordinate online LR, i.e. essentially equal, but LR has half the model size and needs one inner product at prediction time instead of two.

What went wrong
Multiple documented negative results. Boosting gains saturate: almost all NE improvement comes from the first 500 trees, the last 1,000 trees buy less than 0.1%, and for submodel 2 (whose training set is 4x smaller) NE actually regresses after 1,000 trees from overfitting. Global and per-weight learning rates fail outright — global because feature-frequency imbalance shrinks the rate too fast for rare features, per-weight because training terminates too early at a sub-optimal point, making it the worst of the five schemes. The online joiner has a real production hazard the authors call out: if the click stream goes stale due to a data-infrastructure issue, the joiner emits training data with near-zero empirical CTR, the real-time trainer learns to predict near-zero click probability, expected ad value collapses and impressions drop — so an anomaly detector must be able to automatically disconnect the trainer from the joiner. Too short a join window also biases empirical CTR downward by losing late clicks.
Shrinking it to a laptop
One of the best laptop-scale cases in the set. Published at ADKDD '14 (ACM). Everything fits in 8GB.

Airbnb

arXiv paperquality: highreproducible: yescorrected

Problem. Airbnb search ranking had been driven by a gradient-boosted decision tree scoring function that replaced the original hand-crafted one and produced one of the largest booking improvements in company history — but the gains had saturated into long stretches of neutral experiments. The team needed to know whether neural networks could break the plateau on a two-sided marketplace where the ranking model decides which of thousands of listings a guest sees.

Built. A four-stage evolution, each stage A/B tested online against the live model. (1) Simple NN: single hidden layer, 32 fully-connected ReLU units, same features and same L2 regression objective as the GBDT (booked = 1.0, not-booked = 0.0) — deliberately booking-neutral, shipped only to prove the NN pipeline could serve live traffic. (2) Lambdarank NN: switched to a pairwise formulation over {booked, not-booked} pairs from the same search, with each pairwise loss weighted by the NDCG delta of swapping the pair, so rank improvements near the top count more. (3) GBDT/FM NN: fed the factorisation-machine booking probability and the GBDT's activated-leaf index per tree in as features alongside the NN. (4) Deep NN: deprecated all that complexity by scaling training data 10x into a plain 2-hidden-layer DNN — 195 input features after expanding categoricals to embeddings, 127 ReLU units then 83 ReLU units, trained on 1.7 billion pairs. Location preference is handled by hashing {query city, level-12 S2 cell} into a single categorical feature mapped to an embedding, replacing a heavily engineered neighbourhood-booking pipeline.

Outcome. Relative offline NDCG gains and relative online booking gains are published only as unlabelled relative bar charts (Figure 2) — no absolute percentages disclosed. Hand-crafted noise shapes as a dropout alternative gave ~1% offline NDCG but no statistically significant online improvement.

What went wrong
The paper has an explicit FAILED MODELS section and a 'Valley of Despair' retrospective. (1) Listing ID embeddings: the obvious NN win — one embedding per listing, mirroring word2vec and video/user-id recommenders — overfit hard, big training-NDCG gains with none on test. Root cause is marketplace physics, not modelling: a listing can be booked at most 365 times a year, so per-item data is orders of magnitude sparser than for words or videos. (2) Multi-task learning to fix (1): predict bookings and long views from a shared hidden layer with the listing-ID embedding shared, long-view loss scaled by log(view_duration) and the booking loss up-weighted. Online it increased long views by a large margin and left bookings flat — the high-long-view/low-booking listings turned out to be high-priced, hard-to-parse or simply humorous listings. (3) The very first NN attempt fed unnormalised GBDT features straight in and 'went down very badly': loss saturated mid-training and gradients died through ReLUs. (4) Every flavour of dropout tried caused slight degradation in offline metrics. (5) Zero-initialising all weights and embeddings out of habit was 'the worst way to start'. (6) Feature-importance methods all broke: score decomposition is conceptually invalid across a ReLU, ablation hit a Ship-of-Theseus problem because feature redundancy hid single-feature drops in retraining noise, and permutation importance gave nonsense (number of rooms ranked as one of the most important booking features) because permuting correlated features fabricates listings that cannot exist.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Almost every failure in this paper reproduces on 10k rows, CPU-only, and they are the valuable part. Take any tabular ranking set: (a) add a high-cardinality ID embedding and watch train-vs-test NDCG diverge exactly as in their Figure 5; (b) feed unnormalised features to a small Keras MLP and watch the loss saturate, then apply their two transforms — (x-mu)/sigma for normal-looking features, log((1+x)/(1+median)) for power-law ones — and watch it recover; (c) run permutation importance on deliberately correlated features and get the same nonsensical ranking. The 1.7-billion-pair scaling and the 17x Protobuf pipeline speedup do not shrink, but they are the least transferable part of the story.

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: partialcorrected

Problem. ETA prediction underpins pricing, dispatch and delivery promises across rideshare and Uber Eats. The gradient-boosted-tree correction models had grown to the point where accuracy gains required more trees and depth, which directly increased serving latency, and Uber concluded that continuing to scale XGBoost was not feasible.

Decoder is a fully connected network with segment-specific bias adjustment layers. Problem. arXiv paperquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Etsy search suffered a vocabulary gap (buyer query wording differs from seller listing text) and lexical retrieval could not personalise the candidate set, so tail and semantically-phrased queries returned poor candidates before ranking ever ran.

Built. A two-tower embedding retrieval system (Unified Embedding + Personalized Retrieval). Product tower fuses transformer text embeddings, a bipartite query-product graph encoder built from a year of search logs, token/n-gram/char-trigram embeddings, and location encoders (k-means location buckets at k=50/100/500 plus zip-code prefixes). Three-source hard negative mining (in-batch hard, uniform, dynamic hard negatives from the current model) with a warmup schedule that decays uniform-negative loss weight and ramps hard-negative weight. Quality boosting done by concatenating quality features into the vectors (p'=[p;f(p)], q'=[q;w]) so the towers stay independent, with weights found by black-box optimisation. Problem. Podcast search was term-based Elasticsearch, so a query like "electric cars climate impact" returned nothing even though relevant episodes existed in the catalogue, because the episode text never used those exact words.

Built. Natural Language Search: a bi-encoder Transformer in siamese configuration with shared weights, scored by cosine similarity, built on Universal Sentence Encoder CMLM (chosen specifically because it produces sentence-level embeddings directly and covers 100+ languages, matching Spotify's multilingual catalogue). Training pairs came from three sources: successful past Elasticsearch searches, query reformulations following failed searches, and synthetic queries generated by a BART model fine-tuned on MS MARCO. Trained with in-batch negatives (batch of B yields B positives and B^2-B negatives). Served as an additional retrieval source alongside the existing Elasticsearch retrieval, not as a replacement, with ANN indexing in Vespa and a query-vector cache to avoid re-encoding repeated queries.

Stack
Universal Sentence Encoder CMLMBART (fine-tuned on MS MARCO for synthetic query generation)Vespa (ANN index)Elasticsearch (incumbent lexical retrieval)Google Cloud Vertex AINVIDIA T4 GPU

Outcome. The post reports a significant increase in podcast engagement from the A/B test but publishes no percentage or absolute figure - no numbers published for the business metric. The one hard number given is infrastructure: a 6x cost reduction serving query encoding on T4 GPU versus CPU.

What went wrong
The headline admission is that dense retrieval is not a drop-in replacement: it often fails to perform as well as traditional IR methods on exact term matching, and it is more expensive to run on every query. That double failure forced the architectural decision to run it as an additional retrieval source blended with Elasticsearch rather than swapping lexical search out. Manually curated data was only large enough for evaluation, not training, which is why they had to bootstrap positives from search logs and synthesise queries with BART - a data-scarcity problem, not a modelling one.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Fully reproducible at 10k-50k documents on CPU. Take a podcast or article description corpus, index with BM25 (rank_bm25) and with a free sentence-transformers model, then build a deliberately adversarial eval set that mixes exact-term queries (a proper noun, a product code) with paraphrase queries. arXiv paperquality: highreproducible: partialcorrected

Problem. Podcast discovery needs to serve two opposed intents at once - returning to familiar shows and exploring new ones - over a catalogue where new episodes are published continuously and must become recommendable immediately, which breaks recommenders that depend on accumulated collaborative-filtering signal.

Built. GLIDE: semantic-ID-based generative retrieval. Episodes are embedded with multilingual BGE-M3, then discretised into Semantic IDs by Residual K-Means (K=256 clusters, M=4 levels, giving a 1,024-token SID vocabulary). A Llama 3.2 1B backbone is trained in two stages - semantic grounding via bidirectional text-to-SID and SID-to-text translation, then instruction tuning - with LoRA for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Personalisation is injected as a single soft prompt token produced by a two-layer MLP projection of a dense collaborative-filtering user embedding. Multi-task training uses explicit "familiar" vs "unfamiliar" control tokens so exploration is steerable at inference. Candidate generation by beam search with 30 beams. SID collisions (multiple episodes mapping to one SID) resolved by a deterministic popularity tie-breaker over eligible episodes.

Stack

Outcome. 21-day online A/B on approximately 20M impressions: non-habitual streams up to +5.4% (alpha<0.01), new-show non-habitual streams up to +14.3% (alpha<0.01), with GLIDE candidates making up about 34% of recommendations and no regressions in engagement or user satisfaction. What went wrong
First serving attempt did not meet constraints: CPU-side request orchestration became the latency bottleneck under load, and accelerators sat under-utilised because generative decoding is many small sequential steps rather than one big matmul - fixed by scaling the orchestration layer and retuning serving config for 8x throughput. On the quantiser, the fashionable choice lost: the RQ-VAE baseline underperformed and R-LFQ was 4.76% lower on Hit-Rate than plain Residual K-Means, so they shipped R-KMeans for operational simplicity. Greedy decoding was not viable - it cost a 27.07% relative drop in Recall@30 versus beam search. Collaborative-filtering-derived SIDs were rejected outright because new episodes would not be recommendable on publication. Human and LLM-judge evaluation surfaced a failure the offline metrics hid: the recall-favoured model variant had stronger popularity bias despite better offline numbers.
Shrinking it to a laptop
The parts that shrink to a laptop: build semantic IDs yourself. Embed 20k item descriptions with a free CPU sentence-transformer, run Residual K-Means (K=256, M=4) with scikit-learn, measure the collision rate, implement the popularity tie-breaker, and compare against an RQ-VAE - the finding that the simpler quantiser wins is directly checkable. The content-vs-collaborative SID choice reproduces by holding out newly-added items and showing CF-derived IDs cannot address them. What does not shrink: training a Llama 3.2 1B backbone with LoRA, the 20M-impression A/B, and the accelerator-utilisation-under-sequential-decoding bottleneck, which only appears with real GPU serving.

Problem. Stack

Ramp

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Ramp runs a central internal LLM gateway processing trillions of tokens per day across all internal and product AI usage, spread over multiple providers and service tiers whose latency and failure behaviour are non-stationary - they observed, for instance, that OpenAI's flex tier was not always slower than default, and that performance patterns shift across business hours. Built. A dynamic, failure-aware router in the gateway. Failure rates tracked per model/tier with an exponentially-weighted moving average, explicitly excluding failures caused by invalid caller requests so bad clients do not poison a model's score. Latency modelled with Thompson Sampling over a Normal-Inverse-Gamma conjugate prior on log-latency mean and variance, with pooled statistics kept in Redis so all gateway instances share observations. Routing decision made against a per-request deadline using P(bad_outcome) = P(failure) + (1 - P(failure)) * P(latency > deadline), combined with relative cost - so the cheapest model that is likely to meet the deadline wins, and exploration keeps the estimates fresh.

Outcome. Over 25% cost savings without any degradation on the LLM reranker use case (6-second deadline). 30% savings on the streaming equivalent (Ramp Inspect). Peer-reviewed paperquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. LinkedIn's customer support agents had to resolve incoming tickets by finding similar past issues. Historical tickets are highly structured (summary, description, priority, steps to reproduce, root cause, resolution, linked/duplicate tickets), but a plain text-chunking RAG pipeline flattened all of that into arbitrary text segments, so retrieval returned partially relevant fragments and answers were poor.

The naive text-segmentation RAG baseline was the documented failure. Peer-reviewed paperquality: highreproducible: partialcorrected

Problem. Agent benchmarks at the time measured single-turn task completion, which does not resemble deployment. Real customer-service agents must hold a multi-turn conversation with a human, call domain APIs, and obey a written company policy — and must do so consistently, not once in eight tries.

The finding is the failure: agents that look acceptable on a single-shot metric are unreliable in the sense that actually matters commercially. Problem. Building a generative-AI assistant that answers questions on a LinkedIn feed post or job listing (job-fit assessment, company research, interview tips). The team found that evaluating whether an answer was good was harder than building the system, and it became the bottleneck on shipping.

Built. A RAG system with a horizontal platform team and vertical agent teams. Outcome. Reached 80% of their target quality bar in the first month, then spent four more months grinding toward 95%. Invalid LLM-emitted API call parameters dropped from roughly 10% of responses to about 0.01% after the defensive parser. What went wrong
Three documented failures. Reproducible. The 80%-in-a-month / 95%-in-five-months curve is the lesson and it shows up at any scale. Booking.com

Peer-reviewed paperquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Booking.com had deployed roughly 150 customer-facing ML models validated through randomised controlled trials, and needed to answer two questions honestly: does improving a model actually improve the business, and how do you monitor a model in production when you never observe ground truth?

Built. A retrospective analysis, published at KDD 2019, of ~150 production ML applications built by dozens of teams and exposed to hundreds of millions of users, all validated by RCT. Two artefacts matter most for U11. (1) A direct experiment isolating serving latency's business cost. (2) Response Distribution Analysis — a monitoring technique for binary classifiers with no ground-truth labels: instead of measuring accuracy, you plot the distribution of the model's output scores in production. A smooth bimodal distribution with one clear stable point indicates a model genuinely separating two classes; other distribution shapes signal a defective model. This gives you a production health signal without any labels at all.

Stack
randomised controlled trials / A/B testing infrastructurebinary classifiersResponse Distribution Analysis

Outcome. A 30% increase in latency cost approximately 0.5% in conversion rate — described as a relevant cost to the business. The headline finding: increasing the performance of a model does not necessarily translate into a gain in business value, attributed to business-value saturation, segment saturation (limited eligible population), over-optimising a proxy metric such as clicks instead of conversions, and an 'uncanny valley' effect where better predictions unsettle users.

What went wrong
The central negative result: many models that measurably improved on offline metrics produced zero or negative business value in RCT. Teams optimising clicks instead of conversions were optimising the wrong thing. And the uncanny-valley finding — that a more accurate model can make the product feel worse — is the kind of result that only shows up when you insist on RCT rather than trusting offline gains. This is the strongest published evidence against 'ship it, the offline metric went up'.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Highly reproducible and cheap. Response Distribution Analysis is a histogram — students train a small classifier on a 10k-row tabular dataset, plot predicted-probability distributions for a healthy model vs a deliberately broken one (label leakage removed, feature dropped, distribution shifted), and see the bimodal shape collapse. No labels needed, which is the whole point. The latency-vs-conversion lesson reproduces as a simulation exercise: add artificial delay to a local service and compute the conversion cost from the published 30%/0.5% ratio. Airbnb

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: yescorrected

Problem. Airbnb needed to migrate nearly 3,500 React component test files from Enzyme to React Testing Library. The two frameworks differ fundamentally, so no mechanical codemod could preserve test coverage and intent. Manual conversion was estimated at roughly 1.5 years of engineering time.

Built. An LLM-driven migration pipeline structured as a per-file state machine, so every file's progress is independently trackable and the whole corpus can be processed in parallel. When a stage fails, the failure output is fed back into a retry with updated error context. Prompts escalate in context, growing to 40,000-100,000 tokens carrying related source files, the component under test, team-specific test patterns, and existing passing examples. Improvement was driven by a 'sample, tune, sweep' loop: sample the remaining failures, identify the common cause, adjust, re-sweep the whole corpus.

Microsoft (M365 Copilot) / Aim Security

arXiv paperquality: highreproducible: yesverified

Problem. Microsoft 365 Copilot reads a user's email and enterprise documents. Aim Security found that a single crafted email could make Copilot exfiltrate internal file contents to an attacker-controlled server with zero user interaction — the first documented real-world zero-click indirect prompt injection in a production LLM system.

Built. An attack chain (EchoLeak, CVE-2025-32711) that defeats a layered production defence stack, plus a defensive analysis. Chain: (1) attacker sends a benign-looking email carrying a hidden prompt payload, typically an HTML comment or white-on-white text, invisible to the user but ingested and retained by Copilot's LLM context; (2) the payload evades Microsoft's XPIA (Cross Prompt Injection Attempt) classifier; (3) it circumvents link redaction by using reference-style Markdown; (4) it abuses auto-fetched images so the exfiltration request fires without a click; (5) it routes through a Microsoft Teams proxy that the content security policy permitted, escaping the CSP restriction. Net effect: full privilege escalation across LLM trust boundaries with no user action. Recommended defences: prompt partitioning, stronger input and output filtering, provenance-based access control, strict CSP, least privilege and defence in depth.

Stack

Outcome. CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3. Patched server-side by Microsoft; Microsoft confirmed no exploitation in the wild. No numbers on affected tenants published.

What went wrong
Every individual defence Microsoft had in place worked as designed and the attack still succeeded, because the defences were composed rather than layered. A dedicated prompt-injection classifier (XPIA) existed and was bypassed. Link redaction existed and was bypassed by switching Markdown link syntax. CSP existed and was bypassed by finding a first-party proxy already on the allowlist. Auto-fetched images turned a passive render into an outbound network request. The structural failure is that untrusted attacker text and trusted enterprise data shared one context window with one set of privileges — the classifier was treated as the boundary instead of the architecture being one.

Then have students add each Microsoft defence in turn (a classifier, link redaction, an allowlist) and defeat each one, which teaches why the fix is provenance-based access control and prompt partitioning rather than a filter. No cloud, no cost.

Character.AI

Engineering blogquality: highreproducible: partialverified

Problem. Serving conversational LLM inference at over 20,000 queries per second — roughly 20% of Google Search volume — where the average message carries 180 messages of dialogue history. At that scale the attention KV cache dominates memory and I/O, capping batch size and making per-token cost the binding constraint on the business.

Built. A stack of memory-side optimisations aimed squarely at KV cache size, plus a stateful cache across conversation turns. (1) Multi-query attention in all attention layers, giving an 8x KV cache reduction versus the grouped-query attention most open-source models use. (2) Hybrid attention horizons — interleaved local attention with a 1,024-token sliding window and global attention, where only 1 layer in 6 uses full global attention. (3) Cross-layer KV sharing, tying the KV cache across neighbouring layers for a further 2-3x reduction. (4) A stateful caching system that keeps KV tensors in host memory between conversation turns, indexed by a rolling hash with an LRU tree, so a follow-up message reuses the prefix instead of recomputing it. (5) Int8 quantisation trained natively rather than applied post-training, across weights, activations and the KV cache, with custom int8 kernels for matmul and attention.

Stack
multi-query attention (MQA)hybrid local/global attentioncross-layer KV sharingint8 native-precision trainingcustom int8 CUDA kernelshost-memory stateful KV cache with rolling hash + LRU tree

Outcome. Over 20,000 inference queries per second sustained. KV cache reduced more than 20x overall. 95% cache hit rate between conversation turns. Serving cost reduced 33x since late 2022. They state a commercial API would cost 13.5x more than their own serving cost. No quality regression, evidenced on needle-in-a-haystack benchmarks.

What went wrong
Not documented as a failure narrative — the post is written as a success story and explicitly claims no quality regression. The implicit tradeoffs are real though: native int8 training rather than post-training quantisation means the quantisation decision is locked in before training and cannot be undone at deploy time, and using only 1 global-attention layer in 6 is a deliberate long-range-capability bet defended solely by needle-in-a-haystack results, which is a narrow benchmark for that claim.
Shrinking it to a laptop
Split verdict, and worth being explicit with students. Reproducible on a laptop: the prefix/KV cache lesson. Problem. Uber's ETA predictions came from a routing engine plus an XGBoost post-processor. They wanted a deep-learning model good enough to improve accuracy across every business line globally, but ETA is the highest-QPS model at Uber and every prediction has a hard millisecond-scale latency budget on the live request path.

Built. DeepETA, a deep model that post-processes the routing engine's ETA rather than replacing it, with the architecture chosen explicitly for serving latency rather than accuracy. Encoder-decoder with self-attention over tabular features; because a standard Transformer's O(K^2 d) attention was too slow, they adopted a Linear Transformer using the kernel trick to avoid materialising the attention matrix, reducing to O(K d^2). All continuous features are discretised into quantile buckets and embedded; geospatial coordinates use multiple feature hashing with independent hash functions across multi-resolution grids. The decoder is a fully connected network with bias-adjustment layers per trip segment (delivery vs rideshare, pickup vs dropoff). Loss is asymmetric Huber, tunable for outlier robustness and for asymmetric over/under-prediction cost. The serving trick: the model is huge but sparse — any single prediction touches roughly 0.25% of parameters via O(1) embedding lookups instead of O(N^2) dense matmuls, so they hit the latency budget on commodity serving rather than special hardware. Deployed on Michelangelo via the Canvas framework; the uRoute service calls the routing engine then enriches with DeepETA.
